The execution gap: "it had permission" is the wrong question

After an incident, the first question is usually "did the actor have permission?" It is the wrong question. A standing permission is a broad claim about a category of actions. An execution is one specific act. The gap between them is where the damage lives.

Permissions are ambient. Once granted, they hover over every future request an actor might make, waiting to be exercised. They are also coarse by necessity: we grant the permission to "deploy," or "merge," or "transfer," because enumerating every specific deploy, merge, or transfer in advance is impossible. So the permission describes a shape, and any action that fits the shape is allowed to proceed.

Execution is the opposite. It is not a category; it is a single, concrete event with exact parameters — this commit, this amount, this recipient, at this moment. When we authorize the category and let the executor pick the instance, we have authorized the shape and trusted the actor to fill it in honestly. That trust is the execution gap.

The gap is invisible until it is exploited

For a careful human, the gap rarely opens. People fill in the instance the way we expected, most of the time. But the gap is structural, not behavioral — it exists whether or not anyone walks through it. An agent that is confused, adversarial, or simply optimizing against a proxy metric will fill in an instance we never intended, and every layer that checked "did it have permission" will answer yes. The permission was real. The instance was wrong. Both are true at once.

Authorize the act, at the act

The fix is to move the authorization decision from the category to the instance, and from provisioning time to execution time. Instead of a standing permission consulted loosely as actions flow, the actor must obtain authority for the specific action it is about to perform, and present that authority at the moment of execution. The executor does not fill in the instance; the instance is bound into the grant before the executor is allowed to act.

Binding is what closes the gap. In MNDe a grant carries a canonical digest of the exact parameters. The executor validates the presented grant against the action in front of it, and any mismatch — a different commit, a mutated field — produces a refusal before the provider is contacted. There is no "close enough." The authorized act and the executed act are the same act, or nothing happens.

Do not ask whether the actor had permission. Ask whether this exact act carries valid, specific, unconsumed authority — and check it at the act, not before it.

Honest about where enforcement lives

Closing the gap on paper is easy; closing it in production is a systems problem. The binding and refusal logic is implemented and demonstrable. The harder property — that the executor is the only path to the consequential action, so an agent cannot skip the grant and call the provider directly — is target architecture. It holds only when provider permissions, credential custody, and network boundaries are arranged so that the protected executor identity is the sole accepted caller. Until a deployment establishes that, non-bypassability is a design goal, and we label it as one.

None of this makes standing permissions useless. They still gate who may request authority at all. But permission is the outer wall, not the lock on the door. Authorization that fires at the moment of execution, bound to the exact act, is the lock — and it is the part that fails safely when the actor is not a person.
