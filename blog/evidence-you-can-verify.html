Evidence · 2026-06-26

Evidence you can verify without trusting us

Evidence that only means something if you trust the party who produced it is not evidence. The point of a receipt is that a third party can check it independently, offline, against keys they already hold — without taking our word for anything.

Audit logs are usually a matter of trust. The system tells you what it did, you read the log, and you believe it — because inspecting the claim would mean trusting the same system that made it. That circularity is fine until the moment it matters, which is exactly when the system is compromised, mistaken, or under dispute. At that moment a self-attested log is worth very little.

Two receipts, cryptographically linked

MNDe emits two independently signed records for a consequential action. The authorization receipt states what was authorized: MNDe authorized execution X under policy P using grant G. The execution receipt states what happened: executor Y used grant G for execution X and observed result Z. The two are bound together by shared identifiers — the execution id, the grant hash, and the result hash — so that neither can be quietly swapped for a different story. One says what was permitted; the other says what was done; together they say whether those two agree.

Verifiable offline, against published keys

The design goal that makes these receipts useful is independent verification. A verifier fetches the published issuer and executor keys once, and from then on can check any receipt without contacting us — no live service, no privileged access, no trust in MNDe's availability or honesty at verification time. The signatures either validate against the published trust roots or they do not. A broken link or an invalid signature fails verification outright. This is what "without trusting us" means in practice: the check does not route through us.

The test of evidence is not whether we vouch for it, but whether anyone can verify it offline against published keys and reach the same conclusion.

Tamper-evident is not tamper-proof

Precision matters here, and it is easy to oversell. Signed, linked receipts are tamper-evident: alter a receipt and its signature no longer validates, so the tampering is detectable. They are not tamper-resistant in the strong sense — nothing in a signature stops someone who controls the store from deleting a receipt entirely, or from presenting one honest history to you and a different one to someone else. Detecting a deletion or a split view requires more than signatures.

That "more" is transparency infrastructure: independent witnesses and a transparency log that provide inclusion and consistency proofs, so a single party cannot show two different versions of the record without being caught. In MNDe this is target architecture. The receipts and their offline verification are implemented as a limited, demonstrable capability; witnessing and log inclusion are not yet deployed, and until they are, we describe the guarantee as tamper-evidence against a single store, not as protection against a store that lies by omission.

Why be this careful

Because the entire value of verifiable evidence collapses the first time a claim turns out to be softer than it sounded. A receipt that says more than it can prove is worse than no receipt, since it invites reliance it cannot support. Stating exactly what is implemented, what is limited, and what is still target is not modesty — it is what makes the evidence trustworthy in the only way that counts: by being checkable.
